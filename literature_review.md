## Background

The field of automated mathematical discovery has seen significant advances through the integration of machine learning and formal reasoning systems. Building on the work of Beit-Halachmi and Kaminer (2024), mathematical relations can be represented as hypergraphs where vertices correspond to fundamental constants and edges capture their relationships. This framework enables systematic exploration of potential mathematical identities and theorems.

Recent developments by Tsoukalas et al. (2025) have introduced reinforcement learning approaches to evaluate mathematical 'interestingness' - a crucial step in automating theory formation. Their FERMAT system demonstrates how machines can identify promising mathematical patterns and conjectures worthy of further investigation. This connects with Subercaseaux et al.'s (2023) work showing how automated reasoning can both discover and verify results in discrete geometry, particularly in extremal problems involving geometric arrangements.

The integration of natural language processing with formal mathematics, as demonstrated by Welleck et al. (2021) in their NaturalProofs system, has enabled more intuitive interaction with automated theorem provers. This bridges traditional mathematical reasoning with computer-assisted proof techniques, while systems like AlphaEvolve (described by Georgiev et al., 2025) provide frameworks for large-scale mathematical exploration through evolutionary computation.
